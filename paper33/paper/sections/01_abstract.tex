Abliteration, a family of training-free weight edits, can remove refusal
from open-weight language models after release. The developer of
Arbitrary-Rank Ablation (ARA), an optimisation-based method, reports that
direction-based abliteration increasingly fails on newer models, a claim not
yet tested independently. Three direction-based methods (four on the small
pair) and ARA were applied in one framework, with identical prompts, search
budget, divergence cap and selection rule, to an earlier and a later release
of four model families (22--32 billion parameters) and one smaller Qwen
pair, optimising each main-study model--method combination once; an
independent judge labelled refusal on 972 held-out harmful prompts. Averaged
over the direction-based methods, the later release retained more refusal by
+10.7 (Qwen), +4.7 (Gemma), +36.9 (OLMo), $-$1.2 (Mistral Small) and +17.3
percentage points (small pair); after ARA they were +34.4, $-$26.0, $-$2.5,
$-$0.7 and +14.2. The families differed (generation $\times$ family
$\chi^2=547$, 3 degrees of freedom). The later Qwen and Mistral Small
originals already refused more before editing; beyond this baseline
difference (exploratory), the direction-based effect pooled over four
families was +0.06 log-odds, with a 95\% interval of $-$1.67 to +1.80 when the
pair is the unit, including estimated run-to-run variation. At the tool's
100-token window, keyword matching agreed with the judge on average (+0.5
points) but not model by model. Audits of one model pair, removal method or
optimisation run can therefore misjudge how removable refusal is. Refusal
removal did not become uniformly harder in the later releases; its difficulty
depended on the family and the method.
