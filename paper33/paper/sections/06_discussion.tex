\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

% P1: purpose, main finding, take-home (mirrors the abstract)
This study tested whether refusal removal is harder in the newer model of
pairs of open-weight models (H1), whether optimisation-based ARA withstands
any such change better than direction-based methods (H2), and whether keyword
counts agree with an independent judge (H3). With the pair as the unit, the
answer differed by family. After direction-based removal the newer model
retained much more refusal in the OLMo pair (+36.9~pp) and the small pair
(+17.3~pp), somewhat more in Qwen (+10.7~pp) and Gemma (+4.7~pp) and none in
Mistral Small ($-$1.2~pp), and these differences between families far exceeded
prompt-level uncertainty. Beyond the originals' own difference, the pooled
direction-based effect was near zero, but its pair-level interval ($-$1.67 to
+1.80 log-odds) spans large effects of either sign, so it summarises opposing
per-pair effects rather than showing that there is none. ARA lost less
effectiveness than the direction-based methods in Gemma and OLMo but more in
Qwen. At the tool's own 100-token window, keyword matching agreed with the
judge on average but not model by model, and over full responses it counted
more refusals than the judge. Resistance to refusal removal therefore depended
on the combination of family, method and measure rather than uniformly on the
model generation.

% Table tab:pair-effects / Figure fig:heldout-generation / Table tab:h1-heldout: H1
The per-pair effects (Table~\ref{tab:pair-effects},
Figure~\ref{fig:heldout-generation}) differed in sign and size. The OLMo
pair came closest to the account of the ARA author \cite{weidmann2026ara}:
although its originals did not differ, residual refusal on Olmo-3.1-32B rose
by 14.4--61.4~pp under the three direction-based methods, beyond seed noise
for DiM and MPOA, but not under ARA. In the Gemma pair only DiM lost
effectiveness, and its edit of Gemma-4-31B did not reduce refusal (90.1\%
vs.\ 89.3\% for the original); in the Mistral Small pair every method's
effect was smaller than the originals' own difference; and in the Qwen pair
ARA lost the most. The originals' own difference also depended on the item
source: the newer Qwen and Gemma originals refused the code-as-weapon prompts
far more often than their predecessors (+43.2 and +27.0~pp; in Gemma this was
offset by lower refusal on the public sets), whereas the Mistral Small
difference lay mainly in the public sets; the H1 and H2 conclusions were the
same on the public sets alone. One candidate mechanism, shown in one 1B OLMo-2 model, is that more
diverse refusal openings raise the rank of refusal-related activation changes
and weaken single-vector ablation \cite{labunets2026refusal}; it would fit
the OLMo pattern, but rank was not measured here. This pair-specific pattern
resembles non-monotonic black-box safety across Gemma releases
\cite{mitra2026cross} and model-specific abliteration susceptibility across 24
open models \cite{hadetskyi2026not}. To the best of current knowledge, these
are the first held-out, judged estimates of the generation effect for several
removal classes on released model pairs; they show that the verdict of a
comparison based on one pair or one method depends on the pair and the method
chosen.

% Pooled summaries (review revision, author decision A)
The pooled baseline-adjusted effect should be read in this light. With the
prompt as the unit it was +0.03 log-odds with a narrow interval, which could
be mistaken for a precise null; with the pair as the unit it was +0.06 with an
interval from $-$1.67 to +1.80, and a new pair could plausibly show an effect
of either sign. Run-to-run variance hardly changed this pooled interval
because the variation between families was much larger than the seed noise,
but it widened every per-pair interval, so that only four per-pair raw effects
(OLMo and small-pair direction-based increases, the Qwen ARA increase and the
Gemma ARA decrease) had percentage-point intervals that excluded zero once
both sources of noise were included. The baseline
adjustment itself depends on the scale: it changed sign between percentage
points and log-odds in 3 of 21 pair $\times$ method cells, and near 0\%
refusal the log-odds scale gives the Gemma ARA cell, 26.6\% to 0.6\%, the
largest weight. Four pairs cannot represent a population of model families;
they show that the answer to the generational question depends on the family.

% Table tab:h2-contrasts: H2
The pooled support for H2 (Table~\ref{tab:h2-contrasts}) did not generalise
across pairs: with the pair as the unit its interval included zero, and the
prompt-level interaction reversed when Gemma was left out, vanished without
OLMo and was near zero under keyword scoring. ARA's advantage was large where it
occurred (0.6\% residual refusal on Gemma-4-31B, where DiM failed), but on
Qwen3.8-27B ARA left 40.4\%, more than MPOA (14.0\%), which confines support
for the ARA author's claim \cite{weidmann2026ara} to the OLMo and Gemma pairs.
Both ends of this range carry caveats specific to ARA. The Gemma-4-31B export
re-scored above the KL cap, and on Qwen3.8-27B a community model built with
the same defaults reports 0 of 100 dev refusals and refused 26.4\% of the
held-out prompts, against 59--63 of 100 and 40.4\% here. Because ARA's release
step refits its adapter, and refits on a GPU other than the search GPU drifted
more than refits on the search GPU, the ARA cells are less tightly tied to
their searched trials than the direction-based cells. The KL cap also acted
differently on the two classes, binding in every ARA run but in only 15 of
the 24 direction-based runs, and where it bound in both cells of a unit the
interaction was smaller ($-$0.21 log-odds). That the newer Qwen model retained
more refusal under DiM, MPOA and ARA agrees with a report that Qwen3 resisted
abliteration in one setup \cite{agnihotri2025granular}. Prior studies
disagree about whether one direction suffices for Gemma-2: single-direction
edits had little effect on Gemma-2-9B and Gemma-2-27B
\cite{lee2026skindeep,siu2025cosmic}, whereas rank-1 projection sufficed for
Gemma-2-9B within an aligned-minus-base subspace scored by keywords
\cite{nakamura2026measuring}. Consistent with the first two, DiM removed only
14.5~pp of Gemma-2-27B's refusal here. As the first independent evaluation of
ARA, the study shows that its advantage is family-specific and that the step
from searched trial to released model needs its own reproducibility check.

% Tables tab:h3-windows, tab:h3-gap / Figure fig:judge-agreement: H3
H3 predicted that keyword matching would undercount refusal. Pooled over the
ablated models, it did not at any window (Table~\ref{tab:h3-windows}). At the
100-token window that Heretic scores, keyword matching and WildGuard agreed on
average, but they differed significantly, in both directions, in 25 of the 42
ablated models, so the tool's objective was close to unbiased on average and
biased for many single models. Over full responses keyword matching counted
more refusals than WildGuard, because safety and disclaimer markers appear
late in long answers that WildGuard labels as compliance, as an unvalidated
string heuristic suggested for 90.9\% of the keyword-only refusals; this
agrees with reports that disclaimers persist after abliteration
\cite{wuhrmann2026measuring}, that binary refusal metrics miss hedged partial
compliance \cite{weidener2026refusalbench}, and that substring--judge
disagreement on ablated models grows with intervention strength
\cite{rocchetti2026refusal}. The full-response excess is therefore a property
of the window rather than of the tool's objective. The second part of H3 held
in the same form at every window: in the main study the gap was larger for ARA
and on the newer models, with the prompt but not the model as the unit. Keyword matching
tracked the judges on unmodified models, as reported before
\cite{cui2025orbench}, and the large undercount reported in Heretic PR~\#196
was not seen pooled at any window here. Which measure is correct cannot be decided
without human labels: in one hand audit an LLM judge, not substring matching,
over-counted refusal in disclaimer-wrapped answers \cite{rocchetti2026refusal},
safety judges can degrade under distribution shift \cite{schwinn2026coin},
and agreement between judges with correlated errors overstates the evidence
\cite{hossain2026agreement}. The novel finding is that, on edited models of
both generations, the keyword--judge relation changes with the edit, the model
and the length of text read, so keyword-optimised tools are tuned against a
signal whose error differs between models.

% Figures fig:gmpoa-dev, fig:gmpoa-heldout: gamma-MPOA control
The $\gamma$-MPOA control (Figures~\ref{fig:gmpoa-dev}
and~\ref{fig:gmpoa-heldout}) indicates that the post-norm leak is real but does
not explain the main effects. Correcting MPOA for the output gains lowered
refusal on all four post-norm models: on Gemma-2-27B and Olmo-3.1-32B without
a capability change, but on Gemma-4-31B, where the reduction was largest, and
on OLMo-2-32B together with a larger teacher-forced divergence than MPOA and
capability losses of 21.4 and 8.1~pp. The
reductions without capability loss are consistent with the loss of
closed-form edit guarantees in post-normalised blocks
\cite{suresh2026reproducibility} and with norm distortions accumulating across
RMSNorm layers \cite{dang2026selective}; the two others are confounded with a
stronger, damaging edit. The OLMo generation effect (+57.0~pp) and the pooled
H2 interaction ($-$0.42) nevertheless persisted. To the best of
current knowledge, this is the first direct test of the post-norm confound in
abliteration.

% Figure fig:community: community releases
Public releases (Figure~\ref{fig:community}) show that residual refusal under
the KL budget is an upper bound, not a floor. They reached 1.4\% on
Qwen3.8-27B and 0.7\% on Olmo-3.1-32B, where the best models selected here
retained 14.0\% and 10.8\%, at larger teacher-forced divergence but with mean
capability changes within 1~pp. A newer model that is harder at first-token
KL~$\le 0.1$ is therefore not necessarily resistant in practice, in line with the view
that tampering evaluations need strong, tuned attacks to approach worst-case
behaviour \cite{che2025model,hossain2026tamperbench}.

% Figures fig:seed-dev, fig:seed-heldout: seed sensitivity
A second seed (Figures~\ref{fig:seed-dev} and~\ref{fig:seed-heldout}) erased
the small-pair ARA generation effect, so the small-pair H1 verdict depends on
the seed; method nevertheless explained most of the variance among cell rates,
so method rankings are more stable than single-cell generation effects.
Refusal also varies across sampling seeds \cite{larsen2025instability}; the
present data add optimisation-seed variance under greedy decoding. With a
run-to-run standard deviation of about 9~pp per cell, only differences larger
than about 26~pp between two singly optimised models (twice the 12.9~pp
standard deviation of such a difference) clearly exceed the
variation between runs, so the 11 main-study effects inside this band (Table~\ref{tab:h1-heldout})
should be treated as provisional, with the caveat that the band transfers the
seed variance of 4B and 7B models to the 22--32B models
(\S\ref{sec:limitations}). Three of the ten seed pairs also differed in batch
size; without them the band narrowed to 20.4~pp, the same five raw effects
exceeded it, and six rather than three baseline-adjusted effects did.

% Figures fig:capability, fig:divergence: capability and divergence
Capability costs were small for most models but were not bounded by the
selection budget (Figures~\ref{fig:capability} and~\ref{fig:divergence}):
within the restricted range KL~$\le 0.1$ that selection allowed, selected
first-token KL was unrelated to capability change, also among the 42 primary
models alone, whereas teacher-forced divergence tracked it, and the two largest
losses ($\gamma$-MPOA on Gemma-4-31B
and OLMo-2-32B) occurred inside the budget. First-token KL constrains only the
first generated token, and first-token statistics diverge from full outputs in
other settings \cite{wang2024my}. This qualifies the report that refusal
ablation preserves response quality across model sizes
\cite{dombrowski2025safety}: it usually did here, but a first-token budget
could not guarantee it.

% Figures fig:pareto--fig:search-convergence: dev set and search
Dev-set counts (Figures~\ref{fig:pareto}--\ref{fig:search-convergence})
would have suggested different conclusions. On the dev prompts, ARA's selected
keyword count rose by 20.3 refusals on average from older to newer main-study
models, whereas held-out WildGuard judging showed a small pooled ARA
generation effect (+0.12 log-odds, $p_\mathrm{Holm}=0.135$; planned mixed
model +0.15 [+0.04, +0.26]). This contrast mainly reflects the measure rather
than the prompt set: Gemma-4-31B ARA had 28 of 100 dev keyword refusals,
22.3\% held-out keyword refusals and 0.6\% held-out WildGuard refusals, and
under held-out keyword matching the pooled ARA generation effect was +0.57
log-odds. The best dev method was the best held-out method in only 7 of 12
sets, as expected when one noisy criterion is both optimised and used for
selection \cite{cawley2010overfitting}. Search length did not explain the
generation effects: late-improving runs were equally frequent on older and
newer models, and the failed Gemma-4-31B DiM run stopped improving at trial 40.
Re-scoring the released exports on the dev set reproduced the selected
trials' counts to within two refusals for 37 of 46 exports, so the dev values
describe the released models closely; the largest changes were in ARA exports
refitted on a different GPU from the search (\S\ref{sec:res-h2}).

% Hypotheses restated
In sum, H1 was not supported: no main-study pair retained more refusal on the
newer model for every method, the generation effect differed in sign and size
between families, and beyond the originals' own difference the pooled
direction-based effect was near zero with a wide pair-level interval. H2 was
supported in two of four families and pooled with the prompt as the unit, but
not with the pair as the unit, and it was opposed in the Qwen pair. H3's
prediction that keyword counts undercount refusal was not supported, pooled
over the ablated models, at any window; at the tool's 100-token window keyword
counts matched the judge on average but not model by model, and the class and
generation differences in the gap held in the main study with the prompt but
not the model as the unit.

% Methods: confounds pre-empted
Several design features and checks address likely objections. Generation is
confounded with size, data, post-training recipe and architecture, each of
which can change refusal circuits \cite{nguyen2026beyond}; comparisons were
therefore made within family and against each pair's own originals, and they
are stated about the newer model of a pair, because two-point pairs cannot
establish a trend \cite{mitra2026cross}. The post-norm leak was tested
directly. Every model was selected under the same first-token KL cap, but the
cap did not bind in 9 of the 24 direction-based runs, and 8 runs sampled at
most one trial above it; in those 8 the selected maximum weights were not at
the upper bound of the search range, and trials at that bound did no better in
7 of 8, so their residual refusal is the best the search found within the cap
rather than a limit set by that bound, although another strength parameter was
at the strong end of its range in 4 of the 8 and the cap-bound and unbound
units also differ in family (Appendix~\ref{app:kl-cap}). The code-as-weapon bank carried half of the
originals' generation difference, but the conclusions were the same on the
public item sets alone (Appendix~\ref{app:item-source}). Refusal is not
harmful compliance: three quarters of the ablated responses that WildGuard did
not label as refusals were harmful by the HarmBench classifier, yet the pooled
conclusions were the same with harmful compliance as the outcome, although
several per-pair tests changed (Appendix~\ref{app:harm}). A second LLM judge
agreed closely with the primary judge, and no main-study pair met H1 under it
or under full-response keyword matching (with the 256-token keyword window,
all four OLMo methods were higher on the newer model). The small-pair
replicate quantified seed variance, which was used to flag the per-pair
effects that lie within it and to widen the per-pair intervals. Dev-set
selection makes dev counts optimistic, so the hypotheses were tested on
held-out prompts from other benchmarks; excluding the 44 held-out prompts that
match a prompt of the search dataset changed generation effects by at most
1.9~pp. Because first-token KL did not bound capability loss, capability and
teacher-forced divergence were reported for every model. Finally, generation
used one greedy response per prompt with reasoning disabled, and chain of
thought can carry refusal \cite{yang2026beyond}, so the conclusions apply to
greedy decoding in non-thinking mode.

% How the findings fit other research (translation)
These results bear on how open-weight safety is audited. Casper et
al.~\cite{casper2026open} list as an open problem which attack configurations
are needed to stress-test open-weight safety. The present data indicate that
neither one model pair nor one removal method is enough: an audit with DiM
alone would have rated Gemma-4-31B harder than Gemma-2-27B, whereas ARA
removed nearly all of its held-out refusal, and the OLMo and Mistral Small
pairs would have given opposite answers to the generational question.
Defences built around the refusal direction
\cite{yu2025refusal,xie2025beyond,muhamed2026decoy} should therefore also be
tested against optimisation-based removal, and tamper sweeps
\cite{hossain2026tamperbench} should span several model families, method
classes and divergence budgets and treat the family as the unit. For tool
developers, keyword counts and first-token KL are insufficient evidence of
success, and keyword counts should be read at the window used for selection.
Fine-tuning and reinforcement-learning attacks
\cite{qi2024finetuning,russinovich2026grp} were not compared.
